Understanding ICTAD Specifications and Their Role in
Material Testing
🔎 What is ICTAD?
ICTAD (Institute for Construction Training and Development) is the national authority
under the Ministry of Construction and Engineering Services in Sri Lanka. It issues
mandatory technical specifications, standard bidding documents, and rate schedules used in
public infrastructure projects, including road construction.
🛠 Relevance of ICTAD in Road Construction Projects
📑 1. Standard Specifications (SCA/5 & others)
These are standard documents issued by ICTAD to guide construction projects in Sri Lanka
● SCA/5: This contains the technical specifications for building and civil engineering
works. It explains the required quality of materials, construction methods, testing
procedures, and acceptance criteria.
● SCA/4: This document outlines the conditions of contract. It includes legal terms,
contractor responsibilities, payment conditions, and dispute resolution procedures.
● SCA/8: This is the Schedule of Rates. It gives the standard unit rates for various
construction activities and materials, which helps in cost estimation and billing.
These documents define:
● Material quality requirements (e.g., max flakiness index for aggregates)
● Testing frequencies and acceptance criteria
● Tolerances for compaction, layer thickness, etc.
📦 2. Material Selection
ICTAD specifications influence:
● Whether River sand, Quarry dust, or M-sand can be used (based on local
availability and test results)

compliance
📘 3. Frequency & Format of Testing
● ICTAD outlines how often tests (like FDT or sieve analysis) must be conducted

● QA documents and lab reports must follow ICTAD-prescribed formats

Project-Specific Specifications vs. ICTAD
While ICTAD sets baseline national standards, project-specific specifications (from RDA

● Funding agency requirements (e.g., World Bank)

2.Dynamic Cone Penetration (DCP) Test
The Dynamic Cone Penetration (DCP) Test is a widely used in-situ testing method in
geotechnical and pavement engineering to assess the strength and stiffness characteristics
of subgrade and pavement layers. It is particularly valued for its simplicity, speed, and
cost-effectiveness in field conditions.
In this test, a steel cone is driven into the ground by repeated drops of a standard-weight
hammer from a fixed height. The penetration per blow, known as the DCP Index (DPI),
provides a quantitative measure of soil resistance. This index can be empirically correlated
to the California Bearing Ratio (CBR), which is crucial for designing pavement structures
and evaluating subgrade quality.
DCP testing helps engineers identify soil layer boundaries, detect under-compacted zones,
estimate bearing capacity, and monitor compaction quality. Because it requires no borehole,
the DCP test is ideal for rapid assessments across large project sites, particularly for road
work, embankments, and shallow foundation investigations.
While highly effective in granular and stiff soils, the test’s accuracy can be affected by factors
such as rod friction, cone wear, and soil moisture content. Special considerations and
modified correlations are needed for cohesive soils, especially clay types with low CBR
values.
The DCP Test is standardized under ASTM D6951, and its results play a key role in quality
control, subgrade evaluation, and pavement design optimization—making it an
essential tool in modern infrastructure development.

4.Test Pit Analysis (Soil Bag Testing)
📌 Introduction
As part of the site investigation process, soil samples are collected at each DCP test
location through test pits. These samples, referred to as “Soil Bags,” are carefully labeled
and transported to the laboratory for detailed geotechnical analysis.
The primary goal of test pit analysis is to determine the engineering properties of in-situ
materials, evaluate their suitability for use in road construction (e.g., as embankment or sub
base), and ensure compliance with ICTAD specifications.
Each soil bag represents the actual material encountered in the field, making it a critical link
between field observations and laboratory data. By analyzing these samples, we gain
essential insights into:
● Grading characteristics
● Plasticity behavior
● Moisture–density relationships
● Load-bearing capacity
This analysis guides decisions on:
● Material classification (e.g., SB, EMB, SHO)
● Suitability for reuse or replacement
● Stabilization or treatment needs
● Source approval and stockpile categorization

🛣 What is a Macadam Road?
A Macadam road is a type of road built using layers of crushed stones, compacted to form
a hard surface. It was invented by John Loudon McAdam in the early 1800s.
🧱 Layers of a Macadam Road
🏖 Sand Layer (Optional Wearing Course or
Filler)
● May be used as a blinding layer or for
leveling.
● Sometimes skipped or replaced by
bituminous surfacing.
🟡 Prime Coat
● Bituminous emulsion sprayed to bind the
stone base with the next layer.
● Helps prevent dust and water penetration.
🧱 37.5/50/60 mm Stone Layer (Base Course)
● Crushed stones laid and compacted in layers (~100–150mm each).
● Forms the structural base of the road.
🪨 6" X 9" Stone Rubble Layer
● Acts as a foundation or sub-base.
● Large stones manually packed to provide drainage and load-bearing strength.
🟤 Subgrade
● Natural soil at the bottom, compacted to provide support.
● Treated if weak (e.g., with lime or cement stabilization).

Dynamic Cone Penetration
(DCP) Test
📌 Purpose of the DCP Test
The Dynamic Cone Penetration (DCP) Test is
an economical and effective in-situ field method
used to evaluate the strength, stiffness, and
bearing capacity of subgrade and pavement
materials. It provides fast data for quality control,
layer thickness evaluation, and preliminary
pavement and foundation design.
🎯 Key Applications
● Measure in-situ resistance of soil to penetration.
● Evaluate compaction quality of pavement and subgrade layers.
● Estimate California Bearing Ratio (CBR) indirectly.
● Determine soil stiffness, bearing strength, and layer interfaces.
● Detects under-compacted fill in embankments or pavement sections.
● Monitor changes in granular subgrade condition over time.
● Aid in pavement thickness design and foundation suitability checks.
● Support decisions on subgrade stabilization or improvement.
🛠 Equipment Details (Standard DCP Kit Includes)
📦 Standard Components
● Steel Cone: Hardened steel, 60° apex angle, 20
mm diameter.
● Steel Rods: Upper and lower rods, typically 5/8"
diameter.

=== PAGE 45 ===
● Hammer: 8 kg (17.6 lb) dropped from 575 mm (2.26
ft). Dual-mass hammers (4.6 kg + 3.4 kg
detachable).
● Anvil: Transfers energy from the hammer to cone
rod.
● Graduated Vertical Scale: With 1 mm resolution or
electronic reader.
● Accessories: Handle, handguard, 1 m ruler, tommy bars, thread-locking adhesive.
🔁 Hammer System
● Single-Mass: 8 kg – suitable for general soil types.
● Dual-Mass: 4.6 kg (detachable 3.4 kg surcharge). Used for soft soils to avoid
over-penetration.
🛠 Cone Variants
● Reusable cones: Threaded and removable. Replace when worn.
● Disposable cones: Useful in expanding or sticky soils. Detached after testing.
🧰 Additional Features
● Electronic logging systems available for automatic data capture.
● Extension rods allow testing depths up to 1200 mm or 5 feet.
● Apparatus costs range from $1,600–$2,200 USD for manual kits.
🔬 Working Principle
The DCP test is based on measuring soil resistance to cone penetration caused by a
falling hammer. The penetration depth per blow correlates with soil stiffness and bearing
strength, and is used to compute the DCP Index and indirectly estimate CBR and other

Laboratory Tests on Soil Bags (Test Pit Analysis)
Each collected Soil Bag is subjected to a standard series of four laboratory tests, as
required by ICTAD and other road construction guidelines. These tests determine the
fundamental properties of the soil, helping engineers classify the material and assess its
suitability for use in embankment, sub base, or shoulder layers.
1 ⃣ Sieve Analysis (SA) – Grading Test
📌 Purpose:
To determine the particle size distribution of the soil.
⚙ Procedure:
● Dry the sample.
● Pass it through a set of standard sieves (e.g., 37.5 mm, 19 mm, 4.75 mm, 425 µm,
75 µm).
● Weigh material retained on each sieve.
● Plot a grading curve (percentage passing vs sieve size).
📊 What It Tells Us:
● Classifies the material as well-graded, poorly graded, or gap-graded.
● Helps determine if the soil is suitable for sub base or embankment.
● Fine content (passing 75 µm) is important for drainage and compaction.
📚 Specification Reference:
● ICTAD specifies grading limits for SB, EMB, and SHO in terms of % passing each
sieve.

=== PAGE 73 ===
2 ⃣ Plasticity Index (PI) – Atterberg Limits Test
📌 Purpose:
To determine the plastic behavior of fine-grained soil.
⚙ Procedure:
● Conduct Liquid Limit (LL) and Plastic Limit (PL) tests.
● Compute: PI = LL - PL
📊 What It Tells Us:
● Low PI → Soil is non-plastic or slightly plastic (good for compaction).
● High PI → Soil is clayey and may expand/shrink (problematic for subgrade).
PI Range Plasticity Description
0–6 Non-plastic to slightly plastic (good)
7–17 Medium plasticity (acceptable for some fills)
>17 Highly plastic (may require stabilization)
📚 Specification Reference:
● ICTAD often requires PI < 12 for embankment and < 6 for sub base materials.
3 ⃣ Proctor Compaction Test – OMC & MDD
📌 Purpose:
To determine the Optimum Moisture Content (OMC) and Maximum Dry Density (MDD)
for compaction.

Comprehensive Guide to California Bearing Ratio (CBR) Test
🛣 Introduction
The California Bearing Ratio (CBR) Test is a widely used penetration test for evaluating
the strength of subgrade soil and base materials for road and pavement design. It provides a
measure of the load-bearing capacity of soils, guiding engineers in determining the thickness
of pavement layers.
📌 Purpose of the CBR Test
● Determine the bearing capacity of soils used in road construction.
● Classify soils based on strength for subgrade and sub-base layers.
● Provide data for pavement thickness design as per standards (e.g., RDA, AASHTO,
IRC).
🧪 Types of CBR Test
1. Laboratory CBR Test – Conducted on prepared soil samples (remoulded or
soaked).
2. Field CBR Test – Performed in-situ directly on compacted subgrade/base.
⚙ Equipment Required (Laboratory Test)
● CBR mould (150 mm dia. × 175 mm height)
● Spacer disc, collar, and filter paper
● Loading machine (capacity: 50 kN or more)
● Penetration plunger (50 mm dia.)
● Weights for surcharge (if needed)

=== PAGE 79 ===
● Proving ring or load cell
● Dial gauges for penetration and load
● Soaking tank (for soaked test)
● Compaction tools (Proctor rammer, balance)
🧪 Test Procedure (Laboratory CBR)
A. Specimen Preparation
1. Mix soil at desired moisture content (OMC from Proctor test).
2. Compact the soil in CBR mould in three layers using Proctor hammer.
3. Place filter paper and surcharge weights.
4. For soaked CBR: Soak specimen in water for 4 days.
B. Testing
1. Place mould under the loading frame with plunger on soil surface.
2. Apply load at 1.25 mm/min penetration rate.
3. Record loads at every 0.5 mm of penetration up to 12.5 mm.
4. Plot load vs. penetration curve.
5. Calculate CBR at 2.5 mm and 5.0 mm penetration:
CBR=Test loadStandard load×100%CBR = \frac{\text{Test load}}{\text{Standard load}} \times
100\%
● Standard load at 2.5 mm = 1370 kg
● Standard load at 5.0 mm = 2055 kg
● Take higher value between 2.5 mm and 5.0 mm CBR

Comprehensive Guide to Plasticity Index (PI) Test in Road Construction
🧪 Introduction
The Plasticity Index (PI) is a vital parameter in geotechnical and highway engineering. It
measures the plasticity range of fine-grained soils and indicates the soil's ability to undergo
deformation without cracking or crumbling. This guide outlines the complete procedure,
significance, and interpretation of the Plasticity Index Test in road construction.
📌 What is Plasticity Index?
The Plasticity Index (PI) is the numerical difference between the Liquid Limit (LL) and
Plastic Limit (PL) of soil:
PI=LL−PLPI = LL - PL
● Liquid Limit (LL): The water content at which soil changes from plastic to liquid
state.
● Plastic Limit (PL): The water content at which soil changes from semi-solid to plastic
state.
● Plasticity Index (PI): Indicates the moisture range within which the soil remains
plastic.
🏗 Importance in Road Projects
● Helps classify soil using the Unified Soil Classification System (USCS) or
AASHTO system.
● Indicates expansive behavior, which is critical for subgrade stability.
● High PI soils tend to swell/shrink, leading to pavement distress.
● Assists in determining soil suitability for embankments, subgrade, and backfill.
⚙ Equipment Required

=== PAGE 90 ===
● Casagrande liquid limit device
● Grooving tool
● Glass plate for rolling threads
● Spatula and mixing tools
● Oven (105–110°C)
● Moisture content containers
● Balance (accuracy 0.01 g)
🧪 Test Procedure
The Plasticity Index is determined by measuring both the Liquid Limit (LL) and Plastic
Limit (PL) of the soil. These are found through separate tests described below.
A. Liquid Limit (LL) – Identification Process
Purpose: To find the water content at which the soil changes from a plastic to a liquid state.
Steps:
1. Prepare Soil Paste: Take a portion of soil (passed through 425 µm sieve), add
distilled water gradually, and mix into a smooth paste.
2. Fill Casagrande Cup: Place the soil paste evenly into the brass cup of the
Casagrande apparatus.
3. Form Groove: Using a standard grooving tool, cut a straight groove through the
center of the soil.
4. Operate Device: Turn the handle at a rate of 2 revolutions per second so that the
cup repeatedly lifts and drops.
5. Count Blows: Note the number of blows required for the two halves of the soil to
come together for a length of about 13 mm.
6. Repeat: Perform at least 4 trials at different water contents (adjusting soil
consistency).
7. Plot Flow Curve: Plot water content (y-axis) vs. number of blows (log scale, x-axis).

=== PAGE 91 ===
8. Determine LL: Read the water content corresponding to 25 blows from the graph.
That is the Liquid Limit.
B. Plastic Limit (PL) – Identification Process
Purpose: To find the water content at which the soil transitions from a semi-solid to a plastic
state.
Steps:
1. Prepare Soil Sample: Take a small quantity of soil (passed through 425 µm sieve)
and moisten it.
2. Roll Threads: Roll the moist soil on a clean glass plate or tile using your fingers into
threads about 3 mm in diameter.
3. Observe Behavior: If the soil starts crumbling at 3 mm diameter, stop rolling.
4. Collect Sample: Take this crumbled soil and determine its water content by oven
drying.
5. Repeat: Conduct the test with at least three trials and average the moisture content
to get the Plastic Limit.
These two water content values allow you to calculate the Plasticity Index (PI = LL - PL),
which is crucial for classifying and assessing soil for road construction.
🧮 Calculation
PI=LL−PLPI = LL - PL
Example:
● Liquid Limit (LL) = 42%
● Plastic Limit (PL) = 22%
● Plasticity Index (PI) = 42 - 22 = 20%

📏 Test Standards
The Proctor test is standardized in two main versions: the Standard Proctor (ASTM D698,
AASHTO T99) and the Modified Proctor (ASTM D1557, AASHTO T180). Both follow the
same fundamental procedure but with different compaction energies. The Modified Proctor
was introduced in 1958 to cater to higher compaction needs (e.g. for heavy aircraft runways
and highways), because soils that met Standard Proctor densities were found to still deform
under heavier loads. The table below summarizes the key differences between the standard
and modified Proctor tests:

The Modified Proctor uses a hammer roughly twice as heavy dropped from a higher height,
and compacts the soil in more layers. As a result, it delivers about 4½ times the energy of
the Standard Proctor. The increased compactive effort produces a higher soil density and
typically a lower optimum moisture for a given soil . In practical terms, the Modified Proctor
better represents the compaction achievable by modern heavy compaction equipment (and
is often specified for airport pavements, highways, and other heavily loaded structures),
whereas the Standard Proctor is used for lighter compaction requirements (such as
residential or minor works) or as a baseline for general fills. Engineers choose which Proctor
standard to use based on project specifications and the level of compaction needed.

Comprehensive Guide to Field Density Test (FDT) for Road Layers
🚧 Introduction
The Field Density Test (FDT) is a critical quality control procedure used in road construction
to ensure that each compacted layer (such as subgrade, sub-base, and base) meets
specified density requirements. Adequate compaction improves the load-bearing capacity,
stability, and longevity of the road structure. This guide outlines the complete process of
performing and evaluating FDT for road construction projects.
📊 1. Purpose of Field Density Testing
● Verify if the compacted layer meets the specified dry density.
● Prevent structural failures due to under-compacted materials.
● Ensure compliance with project specifications and standards.
🧪 2. Common Test Methods
There are two widely used methods for FDT in road projects:
✅ a. Sand Replacement Method (Standard Method)
Used where the soil is granular or fine-grained. Suitable for embankment, subgrade, and
base layers.
✅ b. Core Cutter Method (For cohesive soils only)
Used primarily in laboratory or for fine cohesive soils in the field. Not ideal for granular or
coarse materials.
🛋 3. Equipment Required (Sand Replacement Method)
● Sand pouring cylinder with calibrated cone
● Metal tray with a central hole (approx. 10 cm diameter)

=== PAGE 155 ===
● Clean, dry standard sand (with known density)
● Weighing balance
● Digging tools (trowel, brush)
● Moisture content containers
● Oven or moisture meter
✅ 4. Test Procedure (Step-by-Step)
a. Preparation
● Level the surface of the compacted layer.
● Place the tray on the surface and cut a clean hole (approx. 10 cm diameter, 10–15
cm depth).
● Collect the excavated soil and place in a container for moisture and weight
measurement.
b. Weighing Operations
● Weigh the sand pouring cylinder filled with standard sand (W1).
● Place it on the hole and allow sand to fill the hole completely.
● Weigh the cylinder again after pouring (W2).
● Also, perform a calibration by filling a standard calibration cone to find the weight of
sand in cone alone.
c. Measurements
● Weight of excavated soil (wet) = W3
● Moisture content = (Wwet - Wdry) / Wdry × 100
● Volume of hole = (W1 - W2 - Wcone) / Sand density
● Dry Density = Weight of dry soil / Volume of hole

=== PAGE 156 ===
🔢 5. Calculations
Let:
● Ws = Weight of dry soil (g)
● V = Volume of the hole (cm³)
Dry Density (g/cm³):
ρd=WsV\rho_d = \frac{Ws}{V}
Degree of Compaction (%):
Degree of Compaction=ρdρd(max)×100\text{Degree of Compaction} =
\frac{\rho_d}{\rho_{d(max)}} \times 100
Where:
● ρd(max)\rho_d(max) = Maximum dry density from Proctor Test
📊 6. Acceptance Criteria
Each road layer has a specific compaction requirement, usually defined as a percentage of
the Maximum Dry Density (MDD) obtained from a Modified or Standard Proctor Test.
Proper compaction ensures the strength and durability of the pavement structure.
Road Layer Minimum Compaction Requirement
Subgrade 90–95% of MDD
Edge Widening Subgrade 95% of MDD
Material Filling
└── Embankment 90–95% of MDD
└── Sub-base 95% of MDD

=== PAGE 157 ===
└── Shoulder 95% of MDD
Aggregate Base Course (ABC Layer) 98% of MDD
🧰 7. Documentation
A typical FDT report includes:
● Location and layer details
● Test method used
● Moisture content
● Bulk and dry density
● Degree of compaction
● Test result (pass/fail)
⚡ Common Issues and Precautions
● Ensure the hole is not too shallow or irregular.
● Use standard, uniformly graded dry sand.
● Protect the test spot from water, vibration, and contamination.
● Check calibration of sand cone regularly.
🔧 Conclusion
FDT is essential for ensuring the structural reliability of road layers. By following the standard
procedure, engineers can confirm whether the compaction meets the design intent, thereby
preventing costly failures and rework.
This guide enables field technicians, quality inspectors, and engineers to confidently execute
and interpret FDT results for road construction layers.

Comprehensive Guide to Concrete Testing in Road Projects
🏗 Introduction
What is Concrete?
Concrete is a composite construction material made by mixing cement, water, fine
aggregates (sand), and coarse aggregates (gravel or crushed stone). It hardens over time
due to a chemical reaction known as hydration, resulting in a strong and durable material.
Ready Mix vs Nominal Mix
● Nominal Mix: A traditional method using approximate volume-based ratios (e.g.,
1:2:4 for M15), commonly used in small-scale works and where high precision is not
critical. In many road projects, especially in minor structures, the Highway Schedule
of Rates (HSR) guideline is followed. HSR provides standard mix ratios and
specifications for commonly used grades of concrete to ensure consistency, ease of
estimation, and suitability for public infrastructure. While it simplifies field batching, it
may lack the rigorous control of a formal design mix.
● Ready Mix Concrete (RMC): A factory-batched and quality-controlled product
delivered to site. The mix is proportioned by weight using a design mix method (e.g.,
M25, M30) and is ideal for road projects due to its uniformity, consistency, and
controlled strength.
Why is Concrete Used in Road Construction?
Concrete is extensively used in road construction due to its high compressive strength,
durability, versatility, and relatively low maintenance. It is ideal for:
● Rigid pavements
● Kerbs and side drains
● Box culverts and retaining walls
● Shoulder slabs and medians
Concrete can withstand heavy loads and adverse weather conditions, making it suitable for
critical structural components in highways and road infrastructure.
Improving Concrete Properties with Chemical Admixtures
To enhance workability, strength, durability, and setting characteristics, chemical admixtures
are added to concrete. Common types include:

=== PAGE 161 ===
● Plasticizers / Superplasticizers: Improve workability without increasing water
content
● Retarders: Delay setting time, useful in hot climates
● Accelerators: Speed up setting and early strength gain
● Air-entraining agents: Introduce microscopic air bubbles to improve freeze-thaw
resistance
● Waterproofing agents: Reduce permeability and enhance durability
These admixtures allow engineers to tailor the concrete mix to suit environmental conditions,
structural requirements, and construction timelines.
Impact of Water on Concrete Strength Water plays a dual role: it is essential for hydration
but excess water weakens concrete. A low water-cement ratio (W/C) increases strength,
while high W/C results in reduced strength, segregation, and increased permeability. A
typical W/C ratio ranges from 0.4 to 0.5 for road concrete.
Effect of Admixtures on Workability Admixtures like plasticizers and superplasticizers
improve workability by dispersing cement particles. This enables easier placement without
increasing water content, thus maintaining strength and durability.
Setting Time of Concrete
● Initial setting time: Approx. 30–90 minutes after mixing
● Final setting time: Approx. 6–10 hours Setting time can be adjusted with admixtures
depending on ambient conditions and transportation delays in ready mix applications.
Testing of Concrete Materials (Before Mixing) To ensure concrete quality, materials must
be tested:
● Cement: Fineness, setting time, soundness, compressive strength
● Fine aggregates (sand): Sieve analysis, silt content, specific gravity
● Coarse aggregates: Sieve analysis, aggregate crushing value, water absorption
● Water: pH level, total solids, organic matter (must be clean and potable)
Concrete Mix Design Concrete mix design is the process of determining the optimal
proportion of cement, water, fine and coarse aggregates to achieve the required strength
and workability.
Steps for Mix Design
1. Define Target Strength: Add a margin (typically 5 MPa) above characteristic
strength.
2. Choose W/C Ratio: Based on strength and exposure conditions.
3. Select Water Content: From IS chart based on slump requirement.

=== PAGE 162 ===
4. Calculate Cement Content: Using W/C ratio and selected water.
5. Choose Aggregate Proportions: Balance based on density, gradation, and
workability.
6. Adjust for Admixtures: Deduct water or cement if using plasticizers.
7. Trial Mix: Prepare sample batches and test slump and strength.
8. Finalize Mix: Based on achieving desired slump and 28-day compressive strength.
Proper mix design ensures durable, economical, and high-performance concrete suitable for
the demands of road construction.
🔢 1. Key Properties of Concrete to be Tested
● Workability

● Consistency

📈 2. Concrete Tests in Road Projects
✅ a. Slump Test – Workability Assessment
The Slump Test is a simple field test used to measure the workability or consistency of fresh
concrete before it sets. It helps ensure that the concrete has the right water-cement ratio and is
suitable for placement, compaction, and finishing on site. The test is widely used in road
construction, building works, bridges, and precast applications as part of routine quality
control.
Why use a concrete slump test?
The main reason to perform the test is to ensure batches of the same concrete are of constant
quality and strength. The more water is added to a mix, the weaker the concrete gets. If
customers require a higher workability/more flowing concrete ( a higher slump result) we
need to increase the cement content to ensure the concrete still reaches its target strength.

=== PAGE 163 ===
This test is just one of the ways we ensure the quality of our concrete. Water is the enemy
when it comes to concrete strengths.
How do you carry out a slump test?
This easy test is carried out in just seven stages, using a slump cone, a steel rod, and a
measuring stick. Here’s how you carry out the test:
· Place the cone on a flat, smooth, horizontal surface and stand on the footholds either side
to ensure the cone is planted firmly on the ground
● Fill the cone in three layers, using the steel tamping rod to compact - or tamp - the
concrete after each layer in an even, uniform manner (this should be done 25 times
per layer).
● Once the cone is filled, remove any overflowing concrete from the top, making sure
the concrete fills the cone exactly to the top level. Remove any spilled concrete from
the base of the cone, too.
● Lift the cone vertically, using a slow and steady motion until the cone is clear of the
concrete. Place the cone upside down on the surface next to the concrete.
● The concrete will subside or slump. To measure the slump, place the steel rod across
the top of the upturned cone so it overhangs the concrete.
● Now, measure from the rod down to the top of the slump. The level of slump is
measured to the nearest 5mm.
Results of the slump test
Depending on the water-cement ratio of the mix, the concrete slump will fall under one of
four categories
● True slump - the concrete largely retains its cone shape, demonstrating that the mix is
cohesive and its workability isn’t too high.
● Zero slump - the concrete retains its shape completely. This shows that the mix is
very dry (this kind of concrete is best used in road construction).
● Shear slump - the top half of the concrete subsides dramatically, leaning to one side,
meaning the mix has workability but low cohesion. The mix may have too much
water content and can be retested after being amended
● Collapse - the mix doesn’t retain its shape at all and completely collapses. This means
the water-cement ratio is too high and needs to be fully amended.

Comprehensive Guide to Flakiness Index Test
🧪 Introduction
The Flakiness Index Test is a standard procedure used in both road and concrete
construction to evaluate the shape characteristics of coarse aggregates. In concrete
applications, aggregate shape significantly influences workability, compaction, strength, and
long-term durability. This test identifies the percentage of flaky particles—those whose least
dimension is less than 0.6 times the mean size. A high percentage of flaky particles can
reduce interlocking, impair cement bonding, and weaken the concrete structure.
📌 Purpose of the Test
● Evaluate the shape quality of coarse aggregates used in concrete and road
construction.
● Ensure aggregates meet flakiness limits for strength, workability, and durability.
● Prevent poor packing and segregation in fresh concrete due to excess flaky particles.
● Maintain reliable bonding between cement paste and aggregate.
⚙ Equipment Required
● Flakiness Index Gauge (Thickness Gauge)
● Weighing Balance (accuracy 0.1 g)
● Standard IS Sieves: 63 mm, 50 mm, 40 mm, 31.5 mm, 25 mm, 20 mm, 16 mm, 12.5
mm, 10 mm, 6.3 mm
● Sample of coarse aggregate (approximately 5 kg)
● Tray and brush
🧪 Test Procedure

=== PAGE 188 ===
1. Sample Preparation:
○ Sieve the aggregate sample into standard size fractions (e.g., 63–50 mm,
50–40 mm, etc.).
○ Discard particles retained on 63 mm sieve and passing through 6.3 mm sieve.
2. Weighing Size Fractions:
○ Weigh at least 200 particles from each fraction (or a minimum of 50 pieces if
availability is low).
○ Record the total weight of particles in each fraction (W1).
3. Flakiness Measurement:
○ Pass each particle from the fraction through the corresponding slot on the
flakiness gauge.
○ Particles passing through the gauge are considered flaky.
○ Collect and weigh all flaky particles (W2).
4. Repeat the process for all sieve fractions.
📐 Calculation

🧪 Introduction
The Ten Percent Fines Value (TFV) Test is a strength test that measures the resistance of
coarse aggregates to crushing under gradually applied compressive load. Unlike the

the TFV test determines the load at which 10% fines (material passing a 2.36 mm sieve) are
generated. It is particularly useful for assessing aggregates used in concrete, asphalt, and
base/sub-base layers.
📌 Purpose of the Test
● Assess the crushing strength of coarse aggregates.
● Identify aggregates suitable for heavy-duty concrete or pavement layers.
● Compare aggregate strength across different sources.
⚙ Equipment Required
● Compression testing machine (200 kN or higher capacity)
● Steel cylinder (150 mm dia., 130 mm height)
● Plunger and base plate
● Weighing balance (accuracy 0.1 g)
● 2.36 mm IS sieve
● Tamping rod
● Oven (100°C to 110°C)
● Aggregate sample (typically 2.8 – 3.2 kg)

🧪 Test Procedure
1. Sample Preparation
○ Obtain aggregate passing 14 mm and retained on 10 mm sieve.
○ Oven-dry the sample at 105°C–110°C and cool to room temperature.

○ For each load, weigh and record the mass of fines generated.

🧪 1. Bitumen Tray Test (Tack Coat Rate Check)
The tray test is used to verify the application rate of bitumen (tack coat) before asphalt is

🧰 Procedure:
1. Place a pre-weighed metal tray (known weight) on the prepared surface before
bitumen spraying.
2. Allow the spray to cover the tray area.
3. Collect and reweigh the tray to determine the amount of bitumen sprayed (by weight
difference).
4. Calculate the application rate (kg/m²) and compare with specifications.

Granular Base (e.g., ABC) 0.6 – 0.9 kg/m²

Marshall Stability and Flow Test

=== PAGE 252 ===
Week 22 – Marshall Stability and Flow Test
This week, I was involved in performing the Marshall Stability and Flow Test, which is a
standard laboratory procedure used to determine the load-bearing capacity and plastic
deformation characteristics of asphalt mixtures. The test is essential for verifying whether
the asphalt produced at the plant conforms to the approved mix design and meets ICTAD
Section 1602 requirements.
🧪 Purpose of the Marshall Test
● Evaluate the strength (stability) of the asphalt mix
● Measure deformation (flow value) under loading
● Ensure the mix has adequate stiffness and flexibility
● Validate production against the design mix performance criteria
🧰 Equipment Used
● Marshall compaction machine (50 or 75 blows per face)
● Mould assembly (diameter 101.6 mm, height 63.5 mm)
● Water bath (maintained at 60°C)
● Marshall testing frame with load dial and flow gauge
🔬 Test Procedure (As per ASTM D6927 / ICTAD Spec)
1. Sample Preparation
○ Asphalt mix was collected from the plant or site.
○ Cylindrical specimens were compacted with 75 blows per face using the
Marshall hammer.

=== PAGE 253 ===
2. Curing
○ Specimens were kept for 24 hours at room temperature.
○ Then immersed in a water bath at 60°C for 30–40 minutes before testing.
3. Testing
○ The specimen was placed in the Marshall testing machine.
○ Load was applied at a rate of 50 mm/min until the specimen failed.
○ Maximum load = Marshall Stability
○ Deformation (in mm) at peak load = Flow Value
📊 Standard Acceptance Criteria (ICTAD / RDA Typical Specs)
Parameter Target Range
Marshall Stability ≥ 9.0 kN (minimum)
Flow Value 2.0 – 4.0 mm
Air Voids 3.0 – 5.0%
VMA (Voids in Mineral Aggregate) 14 – 18%
📋 Site Observations and Results
● The tested asphalt mix (Dense Graded Wearing Course) achieved:
○ Stability: 11.2 kN
○ Flow: 3.1 mm
→ ✅ Within acceptable range
● Each batch was tested during plant trials and pre-laying validations.
● Specimens that failed to meet minimum stability were traced back to bitumen
content or gradation issues, prompting minor plant adjustments.
